When the early Islamic caliphate inherited the administrative traditions of Persia and Byzantium, it also inherited their seals. But the new faith transformed what those seals could show. In place of portraits and pagan deities, Islamic seals showcased something else entirely: the power of the written word.

## The Seal of the Prophet

According to tradition, when the Prophet Muhammad began corresponding with foreign rulers, he was advised that kings only read sealed letters. He commissioned a silver signet ring engraved with "Muhammad Rasul Allah" (Muhammad, Messenger of God)—but crucially, the words were arranged so that "Allah" appeared on top, with "Rasul" (Messenger) below it, and "Muhammad" at the bottom. Even in the Prophet's own seal, God's name took precedence.

This hierarchy became a principle. Islamic seals might bear personal names, religious invocations, or pious phrases, but the design always acknowledged that all authority ultimately derived from divine sanction.

## Calligraphy as Art

Where other cultures might depict images, Islamic artistic tradition elevated writing itself to high art. The Arabic script—with its flowing forms, its capacity for compression and extension, its potential for geometric transformation—became the primary medium for aesthetic expression.

Seal inscriptions employed various scripts:

**Naskh** — The most common book hand, elegant and readable. Our example appears to use this script for its clear, flowing forms.

**Thuluth** — A more formal script often used for titles and architectural inscriptions, with elongated letters and dramatic proportions.

**Tughra** — The elaborate calligraphic monogram used by Ottoman sultans, a masterpiece of interlocking design.

**Square Kufic** — An angular geometric style that could form complex patterns while remaining (theoretically) legible.

## The Choice of Carnelian

Islamic seals favored carnelian above almost all other stones. This wasn't merely aesthetic—a hadith (saying of the Prophet) reportedly endorsed the red-orange stone, and the Prophet's own ring was said to be carnelian.

The stone carried additional significance: carnelian was believed to bring good fortune, to calm anger, and to protect against the evil eye. When set in silver (as in our example), it combined these properties with silver's traditional associations with purity and the moon.

The deep red-brown color of our seal's carnelian exemplifies the finest Persian material, prized for its intensity and clarity.

## The Unexpected Bird

Look at the top of our seal and you'll see something surprising: a figural element in the form of a long-billed bird, possibly an ibis. This challenges the common assumption that Islamic art avoided all figural representation.

The reality was more nuanced. While figurative imagery was prohibited in religious contexts (mosques, Qurans), secular objects like seals, textiles, and metalwork often included animals, birds, and even human figures. This was particularly true in Persian and Ottoman court art, where pre-Islamic traditions persisted alongside Islamic ones.

The bird atop our seal might have symbolic meaning—the ibis was associated with wisdom and writing in earlier Near Eastern traditions—or it might simply be decorative, an artisan's flourish on a luxury object.

## Function and Form

Despite their beauty, these seals served practical purposes. The owner would press the carnelian face into warm wax or damp clay, leaving an impression that authenticated a document. The inscription—typically the owner's name, perhaps with their father's name, and often a pious phrase—became their legal signature.

The silver mount served multiple functions:

- **Protection** for the delicate stone
- **A handle** for pressing the seal
- **Display** of wealth and taste through elaborate metalwork
- **Suspension** via the attached loop, allowing the seal to be worn or carried

The arabesque patterns and openwork on the silver weren't mere decoration—they advertised that this seal (and its owner) belonged to the cultured elite.

## Continuity Across Empires

Our seal, dating somewhere between 1600 and 1900 CE, could be Ottoman or Safavid—the traditions overlapped enough that attribution remains uncertain without provenance. This ambiguity itself tells a story: despite political rivalry between the Ottoman and Safavid empires, they shared a common Islamic visual language.

From the earliest caliphal seals to the last Ottoman tughra, the principle remained constant: in the Islamic world, the word was the image. A beautiful seal wasn't one that showed its owner's face but one that rendered their name with grace, set it in precious materials, and acknowledged—even in so personal an object—that all authority, all identity, all meaning ultimately derived from the divine word that shaped the cosmos.
